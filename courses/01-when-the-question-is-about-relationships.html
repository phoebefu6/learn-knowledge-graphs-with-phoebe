When the question is about relationships

"Which Nobel laureates studied under someone who also won?" is not a lookup. It is a question about a relationship between two people, and about a property of each. Tonight we name the parts (entity, relation, hop), learn to spot the question a table answers badly, and see what goes quietly wrong when you answer it by retrieving chunks of text instead of walking a graph. The graph is real: Wikidata, around a thousand laureates.

Part 0

Where we start

Most questions people bring to data are about one thing at a time: this customer's balance, that paper's year. Some are about how things connect, several steps away. Those are the questions a knowledge graph exists for, and the ones a pile of retrieved text answers worst, without telling you. Across six sessions one constructed publisher, Vellum Press, builds a graph design note one section at a time, from tonight's question sort to a costed decision on graph, vectors or both.

Part 1 · covers entity, relation, hop

Entities, relations and the hop

5 min live

A knowledge graph stores things (entities) and the typed connections between them (relations). A hop is one step along one relation. The number of hops a question needs is the single most useful fact about it, because it decides which tools can answer it at all.

LiveEntity, relation, hop: the three words you need3 min▶

An entity is a thing with an identity: a person, a university, a prize. A relation is a typed, directed connection between two entities: ADVISED, EDUCATED_AT, WON. Typed matters: "Banerjee advised Duflo" and "Banerjee worked with Duflo" are different edges, and a question can ask for one and not the other.

A hop is one traversal of one relation. "Who was Duflo's advisor?" is one hop. "Which prize did Duflo's advisor win?" is two. The figure's question is four. Count the hops before you choose a tool: almost every retrieval method handles one hop, and the methods diverge sharply after that.

Hogan and colleagues' survey gives the working definition used across these pages: a knowledge graph is "a graph of data intended to accumulate and convey knowledge of the real world", whose nodes are entities and whose edges are relations between them. They call it deliberately inclusive.

Self-studyWhy a hop count is a property of the question, not the data3 min▶

The same graph answers one-hop and four-hop questions equally well, because traversal is what it stores. The hop count lives in the question. That is why the build-along tonight sorts questions, not tables: the sort tells you whether you need a graph, before anyone builds one.

Two traps when counting. First, a hidden hop: "laureates from the same university as their advisor" reads like one relation but needs three (person to advisor, person to university, advisor to university). Second, a fake hop: "papers by Duflo" is one hop even if the word list is long. Write the relations out and count the arrows.

Real world

Wikidata itself. Wikidata is a public knowledge graph: every item has a Q-number (Esther Duflo, Abhijit Banerjee and the prize all have one), every relation is a numbered property (P184 doctoral advisor, P166 award received), and its structured data is released under CC0. Everything on these pages is built from it, which is why every name here can be checked at source.

Part 2 · covers tables vs graphs, join depth as the tell

Join depth is the tell

5 min live

Any graph fits in a table: one row per edge, with a source, a type and a target. Then every hop becomes one more self-join. One or two joins is fine. When the number of joins depends on the data ("advisors, all the way up"), SQL needs recursion, and the query stops describing the question.

LiveOne self-join per hop3 min▶

Put the graph in a relational database as edge tables and the hop count becomes the join count. A two-hop question is one join, perfectly ordinary SQL. A four-hop question is three self-joins on the same table, still possible, already hard to read and easy to get subtly wrong (join the wrong column and you walk students instead of advisors).

The real break is a variable depth: "every academic ancestor of Duflo". SQL can do it with a recursive common table expression, and the query no longer looks like the question. A graph query writes the depth as a number, *1..4, and session 3 shows both side by side.

LiveWhen a graph beats a table, and when it does not3 min▶
The question looks likeBetter home
Totals, filters, group-bys over many rows of one kindA table (the warehouse)
One or two fixed joins between well-known entitiesA table; a graph adds little
Paths of varying length, "connected to", "anywhere upstream"A graph
Many relation types between few kinds of entity, changing oftenA graph (adding an edge type is not a migration)
Who sits between groups, who is centralA graph and its algorithms (session 5)

Relational modelling itself, keys, grain and normal forms, is taught in Data Modeling, ER, cardinality and keys ↗. If your question is about lineage between data assets rather than knowledge about the world, start with Metadata, Catalog and Lineage, session 3 ↗.

Real world

Even a graph store caps the depth. The public Wikidata Query Service stops any query at 60 seconds, by its own user manual. Deep, unanchored path queries over a graph of its size hit that wall. Anchoring the pattern on one named entity, as every question on the bench does, is what keeps multi-hop queries cheap.

Part 3 · covers what chunk retrieval silently gets wrong

What chunks get wrong, quietly

5 min live

Retrieval over text chunks finds passages that share words with the question. The first hop shares the question's words: the person is named. The later hops do not. The bridge people, Banerjee, Maskin, Arrow, never appear in the question, so their passages rank like any other passage that mentions "doctoral advisor".

LiveThe bridge entity is not in the question3 min▶

Keyword retrieval (BM25) scores a chunk by how many of the question's rarer words it contains. "Duflo" is rare, so the chunk naming her advisor ranks first. The chunk "Abhijit Banerjee's doctoral advisor was Eric Maskin" contains none of the rare words. It matches only "doctoral advisor", which hundreds of chunks share, and lands at rank 350. Embedding retrieval softens the vocabulary match but not the problem: the question still does not mention Banerjee.

There is a worse twist. The top chunk on this question says Duflo herself won the Economics prize in 2019. A reader given only the top chunks can produce the right prize name for the wrong reason, and look correct. On the bench, a question only counts as found when every fact on the answer path was retrieved.

Self-studyWhat the bench measures, and what it does not claim4 min▶

Session 4's bench asks 24 questions, six each at one to four hops, two ways. Graph traversal finds all 24. BM25 over two-fact chunks at k = 10 finds 9: all six one-hop questions, three of the six two-hop ones, none at three or four hops. Raising k to 30 triples the text sent per question (174 words to 530) and finds the same 9.

The baseline is deliberately simple: single-shot keyword retrieval over sentences generated from the same facts the graph holds. Vector retrieval and rerankers are taught in the RAG course ↗, and multi-step retrieval that re-queries with the names it found would close part of the gap. The point is the mechanism, not a league table.

Real world

Benchmarks built for exactly this. Multi-hop question answering has its own datasets because single-passage retrieval fails on it. HotpotQA (2018) collected 113k questions that need reasoning over multiple supporting documents; MuSiQue (2022) composed 25K questions of two to four hops from single-hop ones, so a model cannot shortcut them. Session 6 reads what graph-based retrieval does and does not win on such benchmarks.

Then you run the desk's headline question as one pattern in the query box below, on the real graph. The output is section 1 of Vellum's graph design note.

Write the ten questions down as the desk asked them. Plain words, no tool in mind yet. These are Vellum's, constructed for these pages; every one has a real answer in the graph.

Rewrite each as entities and relations. Name the anchor entity, then the chain of relation types, then what comes back. Use only five relation types: WON, ADVISED, EDUCATED_AT, EMPLOYED_BY, CITIZEN_OF.

(any prize) <-WON- advisor -ADVISED-> student -WON-> (any prize) no anchor: the pattern runs over the whole graph

Count the arrows and sort. One arrow is one hop. Put each question in a column, as in the figure. Mark the ones with no named anchor: they are whole-graph questions, the most expensive kind.

Run the headline question. The query box below runs a small, documented subset of Cypher on the real graph, in your browser. Click "Laureates whose advisor also won" and read the row count. Then try the Duflo chain and change *3 to *2: you have just asked a different question.

Write section 1 of the design note. Three lines are enough. Keep the question numbers: session 3 turns four of them into queries, session 4 measures them.

★ Step 5 · Vellum graph design note, section 1Question sort: 3 of 10 desk questions are one hop; 7 need 2 to 4. The headline question is a 3-hop whole-graph pattern: 191 laureates. Decision so far: the multi-hop questions are the reason for a graph; the one-hop ones would be fine in the existing database.

An empty result is not a "no". If a pattern returns nothing, the graph may simply be missing the edge. Wikidata records doctoral advisors for some laureates and not others. Session 4 makes that failure measurable: drop one edge type and watch the graph answer fewer questions without a single error.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · "Which Nobel Prize did Max Delbrück's doctoral advisor win?" How many hops is it?

Hops count relations traversed, not entities named. Delbrück <-ADVISED- Max Born -WON-> Physics prize is two arrows. The number of prizes changes the number of rows, not the hops.

2 · Why does keyword chunk retrieval find the first hop of the Duflo question but not the later ones?

The first fact names Duflo, a rare word in the question, and ranks 1. The bridge facts share only "doctoral advisor" with the question, like hundreds of other chunks, and rank 350, 330 and 313. Every fact is in the corpus; retrieval cannot reach it.

3 · Which question is the strongest sign that a graph will earn its keep?

A variable-depth path is the case where SQL turns into recursion and chunk retrieval loses the chain. B and D are aggregates and filters over one kind of row, a table's home ground; C is a single hop any method answers.
