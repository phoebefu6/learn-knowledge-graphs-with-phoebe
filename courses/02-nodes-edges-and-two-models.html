Part 1 · covers the labelled property graph

The property graph: labels, properties, typed edges

5 min live

A labelled property graph has nodes and directed, typed relationships. Every node carries a label (Person, Prize) and a bag of key-value properties. So does every relationship. That last part is the whole trick: the year Max Born won sits on the WON edge itself, because it is a fact about the winning, not about Born or about the prize.

LiveNodes, labels and properties3 min▶

A node is one entity. Its label says what kind of thing it is: Person, Institution, Country, Prize. Its properties are the plain values you would otherwise put in columns: a name, a Wikidata id, a birth date. A relationship joins exactly two nodes, has one type (WON, ADVISED) and a direction.

Hogan and colleagues' survey, read at source, defines this model precisely: a property graph "allows a set of property-value pairs and a label to be associated with both nodes and edges". Two simpler shapes sit underneath it in the same section. A directed edge-labelled graph is just nodes and labelled arrows, nothing hanging off either. A heterogeneous graph gives each node and edge one type, as part of the model rather than as an extra relation. The property graph adds the key-value pairs.

LiveProperties on edges: why the year sits on WON3 min▶

Try the alternatives. Put year: 1954 on Max Born and it breaks the moment someone wins twice: Marie Curie won Physics in 1903 and Chemistry in 1911, and in the extract she has two WON edges. Put the year on the Physics prize node and it is nonsense, because the prize was awarded every year. The year describes one person winning one prize, which is exactly what one edge is.

The test generalises. When a value only makes sense for a pair of entities (a year, a role, a start and end date, a share), it belongs on the edge. When it makes sense for one entity alone (a name, a birth date), it belongs on the node.

Self-studySchema-first vs schema-later4 min▶

Most property graph stores let you write any label and any property without declaring them first: schema-later. You load the data, then add constraints (this label must have a unique qid) as you learn what you need. Relational databases are schema-first: the table exists before the row.

Schema-later is why adding a new relationship type is not a migration, the point session 1 made. It is also why a typo becomes a new type silently: ADVISOR next to ADVISED and half your edges vanish from every query. The build-along tonight writes the schema down anyway, as a short list in the design note, because a model nobody wrote down is a model nobody can check.

Real world

The property graph got an ISO standard. ISO/IEC 39075:2024, "Database languages - GQL", was published 2024-04-12 as an International Standard. It defines data structures and operations on property graphs and a language to create, query and maintain them, for portability across implementations. (Read at the IEC webstore; iso.org refused our automated fetch, so ISO's own catalogue page is cited as reported.) A sibling standard, ISO/IEC 9075-16:2023, SQL/PGQ, is reported as defining how SQL itself represents and queries property graphs. Session 3 shows where Cypher already matches GQL and where it does not.

Part 2 · covers RDF triples, IRIs, literals, blank nodes

RDF: everything is a triple

5 min live

RDF writes every fact as one triple: subject, predicate, object. Born, award received, Physics prize. Every thing is named by a global IRI, so two datasets that use the same IRI are talking about the same thing. The catch is in the shape: a triple has three slots and no fourth. There is nowhere to put the year.

LiveSubject, predicate, object, and the IRI3 min▶

The RDF 1.1 Primer (W3C Working Group Note, 24 June 2014) puts it in one line: "An RDF statement expresses a relationship between two resources." The subject and object are the resources, the predicate is the relationship. Max Born, award received, Nobel Prize in Physics.

Each of those is an IRI, a global identifier: "An IRI identifies a resource". Wikidata's IRI for Born is http://www.wikidata.org/entity/Q58978, shortened to wd:Q58978 with a prefix. Because the identifier is global, anyone else's dataset that says wd:Q58978 is talking about the same physicist, not a namesake. That is the property graph's qid property promoted to the identity of the node itself.

Note what the predicate is: also an IRI, not a thing with slots. In a property graph the WON edge is an object you can hang {year: 1954} on. In plain RDF, wdt:P166 is a name in the middle position, and the triple is complete.

Self-studyLiterals only as objects; blank nodes4 min▶

Literals are the plain values: strings, numbers, dates. The Primer: "Literals are basic values that are not IRIs", and they "may only appear in the object position of a triple." So "1954" can be the end of a triple, never the start of one. You cannot say anything further about a literal.

Blank nodes are resources without a global identifier, "like simple variables in algebra"; they can be a subject or an object. They are how RDF says "there is a something here" without naming it, which is exactly what the year problem needs: a something that stands for "this particular award", with the person, the prize and the year all attached to it. The figure's _:st is one, standing in for Wikidata's statement node.

Real world

RDF 1.2 is being finished as we write. The W3C's RDF 1.2 Concepts document is a Candidate Recommendation Draft dated 16 November 2026, not yet a Recommendation. Its headline addition is the triple term: a triple can be used "as a triple term, in the object position of another triple", which gives plain RDF a direct way to say something about a fact, such as the year it happened. Until it is a Recommendation, the RDF 1.1 Primer of 2014 is the stable reference, and statement nodes are what large graphs like Wikidata actually use.

Part 3 · covers Wikidata statements, qualifiers, references, ranks

How Wikidata does both

5 min live

Wikidata is RDF underneath, but its unit is not the triple. It is the statement: one property-value pair on an item, which can carry qualifiers (the year), references (the source) and a rank (how much to trust it). Every statement is published twice, once as a short truthy triple and once as a full statement node. Knowing which one you queried is the difference between "Born won Physics" and "Born won Physics in 1954".

LiveItems, properties, statements, qualifiers3 min▶

An item is an entity with a Q-prefixed id: Max Born is Q58978. A property is a P-prefixed id: P166 award received, P184 doctoral advisor, P585 point in time. Wikidata's help pages: "A statement consists of a property-value pair." Born's award statement is P166 = Q38104.

A qualifier "further describes or refines the value of a property given in a statement". The award year is a qualifier: P585 = 1954, on the P166 statement. It plays exactly the role of {year: 1954} on the WON edge. References "point to specific sources that back up the data provided in a statement". Ranks say how much to trust it: preferred (most current, best consensus), normal ("assigned to all statements by default") and deprecated (statements "known to include errors").

In SPARQL this gives two routes. wdt:P166 is the truthy shortcut: item straight to value, best non-deprecated rank only (preferred if any exist, otherwise normal), qualifiers ignored. The full route is p:P166 to the statement node, ps:P166 to the value, pq:P585 to the qualifier. Vellum's extract used the full route, because it needed the year.

LiveQIDs settle who is who3 min▶

Names collide. In Vellum's extract, 8 institution labels are shared by two different items, "King's College" and "University of Strasbourg" among them. Key the graph on names and those pairs merge into one node, and every EDUCATED_AT edge into either goes to the wrong place without an error. Key it on QIDs and they stay apart. The model below makes qid the key on every node for that reason, with name for people to read.

Here identity came free, because every record arrived with a QID. When your sources share no key at all, deciding which records are the same entity is its own discipline, taught in Entity Resolution, session 1: no shared key ↗. Do that first, then build the graph on the identifiers it produces.

One more identity lesson from the build. Many items now carry their English name only as a "mul" label (default for all languages). An English-only label request returned no name for Marie Curie (Q7186); asking for English with a mul fallback fixed it. 103 entities in the extract carried only a mul label. Without the QID, those would have been nameless nodes.

Self-studyThe graph is only as true as its edges4 min▶

A real artefact in the extract, checked in the raw data: Theodor Zincke (Q90591) lists Otto Hahn (Q57065) as both a doctoral advisor (P184) and a doctoral student (P185), while Hahn lists Zincke as his advisor. So Vellum's graph holds a two-node loop: Hahn advised Zincke, and Zincke advised Hahn. Any "academic ancestors, all the way up" query that walks into it will go round. The graph did exactly what it was told; the edge is the problem.

The opposite failure is the missing edge. Hogan and colleagues describe the Open World Assumption: absence of an edge does not mean the fact is false, since it "could be part of some knowledge not (yet) described by the graph". A Closed World Assumption treats unknown as false. Read a Wikidata extract open world. So when a query for Marie Curie's laureate students returns 0 rows in this extract, the honest reading is "not recorded here", not "none".

Real world

Wikidata's licence and its ranks. Structured data in Wikidata's main, Property and Lexeme namespaces is released under CC0, which is why Vellum can copy it into a private graph without attribution terms; text in other namespaces is CC BY-SA. And the deprecated rank is Wikidata's own mechanism for keeping a known-wrong statement visible but out of the truthy view. The extract skipped deprecated statements, so a statement the community has marked as wrong never became an edge here.

Build-along

Vellum's graph model

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Five steps, no install. You take the ten desk questions from session 1, pull out every kind of entity and relation they mention, and fix the model: labels, key properties, relationship types, directions, and where the year goes. Then you check the model against the real graph in the query box and write section 2 of the design note.

List the entities in the ten desk questions. Go back to session 1's list and underline every noun that is a thing with an identity. Group them by kind. Those kinds are your node labels.

★ Step 1 · entities, groupedPeople ........ Dorothy Hodgkin, Max Born, Niels Bohr, Max Planck, Max Delbrück, Ernest Rutherford, Enrico Fermi, Esther Duflo, and every advisor and student between Prizes ........ the six Nobel prizes (Physics, Chemistry, Medicine, Literature, Peace, Economic Sciences) Institutions .. "where was Bohr educated?" Countries ..... "which country was Bohr's advisor a citizen of?" Not entities .. years, birth dates: values, so properties

Choose labels and the key property. One label per kind. Every node gets qid as its key and name for display. Remember the 8 institution labels shared by two items: a name is not a key.

★ Step 2 · node labels(:Person {qid, name}) key: qid (:Institution {qid, name}) key: qid (:Country {qid, name}) key: qid (:Prize {qid, name}) key: qid Why qid: "King's College" names two different items in the extract.

Choose relationship types and their direction. One type per Wikidata property used. Direction is a decision, not a detail: ADVISED points from advisor to student, so "Duflo's advisors" walks the arrow backwards and "Planck's students" walks it forwards. Write the direction down once, or every query author will guess.

★ Step 3 · relationship types(:Person)-[:WON {year}]->(:Prize) from P166 + P585 (:Person)-[:ADVISED]->(:Person) advisor -> student from P184 and P185 (:Person)-[:EDUCATED_AT]->(:Institution) from P69 (:Person)-[:EMPLOYED_BY]->(:Institution) from P108 (:Person)-[:CITIZEN_OF]->(:Country) from P27 Wikidata stores both P184 (my advisor) and P185 (my student); the model keeps ONE edge type and one direction for both.

Decide where the year goes, and write the same fact both ways. In the property graph it is an edge property. In Wikidata it is a qualifier on the statement. Compare the two SPARQL forms below: the truthy one cannot return the year at all.

★ Step 4 · one fact, three writingsProperty graph: (:Person {qid:"Q58978", name:"Max Born"}) -[:WON {year:1954}]->(:Prize {qid:"Q38104"}) Wikidata, truthy (no year possible): SELECT ?prize WHERE { wd:Q58978 wdt:P166 ?prize . } Wikidata, full statement (keeps the qualifier): SELECT ?prize ?when WHERE { wd:Q58978 p:P166 ?st . ?st ps:P166 ?prize . OPTIONAL { ?st pq:P585 ?when . } }

Self-study: run both at query.wikidata.org. Paste each query into the public Wikidata Query Service and compare the columns. No outputs are printed here, because Born's award list on Wikidata is live data and will not match a snapshot; what matters is that only the second query has a ?when column to fill.

Write section 2 of the design note. Check the model against the real graph first: click the two buttons in the query box below. Marie Curie should come back with two prizes, Physics and Chemistry, which proves WON is one edge per award and not one per person. Max Born's students and grand-students who won should come back as 4 rows, which proves ADVISED points the way you decided.

★ Step 5 · Vellum graph design note, section 2Model: 4 node labels, 5 relationship types, all keyed on Wikidata QID. Person 1,523 · Institution 1,837 · Country 141 · Prize 6 WON 1,005 (year on the edge) · EDUCATED_AT 3,566 · EMPLOYED_BY 3,413 ADVISED 966 (advisor -> student) · CITIZEN_OF 2,004 Checks: Marie Curie WON -> 2 rows; Max Born ADVISED*1..2 then WON -> 4 rows. Watch: Zincke and Hahn advise each other (a two-node loop from the data); cap variable-length ADVISED walks.

Flip an arrow and see the model bite. In the Born pattern, change -[:ADVISED*1..2]->(s) to <-[:ADVISED*1..2]-(s). You are now asking for Born's advisors and their advisors who won, a different question with a different answer. Direction is part of the meaning, which is why step 3 wrote it down.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Max Born won the Physics prize in 1954. Where does the year belong, in a property graph and in Wikidata?

The year describes one person winning one prize, so it sits on the edge. Wikidata does the same job with a statement node: P166 = Q38104, qualified by P585 = 1954. On the person it breaks for Marie Curie, who won twice; on the prize node it is meaningless.

2 · What is a qualifier in Wikidata?

Qualifiers "further describe or refine the value of a property given in a statement". C is a reference; D is the deprecated rank. And the truthy wdt: shortcut ignores qualifiers, so you need p:, ps: and pq: to read one.

3 · Why does Vellum's model key every node on its QID rather than its name?

"King's College" names two different items in the extract. Keyed on names, their edges would land on one node without any error. Names stay as a display property. When your sources have no shared id at all, entity resolution comes first.
