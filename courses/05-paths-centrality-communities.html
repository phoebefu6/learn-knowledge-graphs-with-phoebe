Part 0

Where we start

A pattern query answers the question you wrote. A graph algorithm answers a question about the whole shape: the shortest route between two nodes, the nodes everything flows through, the clusters that hang together. These are the answers editors love to quote, and the ones most sensitive to a modelling choice nobody wrote down. Tonight you compute each one twice, on two views of the same graph, and learn to say which view the number came from.

Part 1 · covers shortest paths and what they answer

The shortest path depends on the edges you allow

5 min live

A shortest path answers "how are these two connected, in the fewest steps?". On a graph without weights every edge costs one, so the answer is a hop count and one route. The catch: the route is only as meaningful as the edges you let it use. Allow a prize node and two economists who never met are two hops apart.

LiveWhat "shortest" means when edges have no weight3 min▶

Without weights every edge counts as one step, so the shortest path is the one with the fewest hops. Breadth-first search finds it: visit every neighbour of the start, then every neighbour of those, ring by ring, and stop the moment the target turns up. The first ring that contains the target is the hop count.

In networkx the call is shortest_path(G, source=None, target=None, weight=None, method='dijkstra'). With weight=None every edge counts 1 and the unweighted method is used; give it a source and a target and you get one list of nodes back. "One" matters: several routes can tie on length, and the function returns just one of them. The widget below does the same, a breadth-first search over neighbours in a fixed order, so it always shows the same route.

What a shortest path answers well: "are these two connected at all, and how closely?". What it does not answer: "how strongly", "how often" or "through whom, typically". A single route hides every other route of the same length.

LiveThe path you get depends on which edges you allow3 min▶

On the advisor network (people only, ADVISED edges only) Esther Duflo reaches Paul Samuelson in 7 hops. The route climbs three generations to Kenneth Arrow, steps down to Arrow's student Michael Spence, climbs through Spence's other advisor Thomas Schelling to Wassily Leontief, and steps down to Leontief's student Samuelson. Every hop is a real doctoral relationship, and the route tells a story an editor could check.

On the full graph, with institutions, countries and prizes as nodes, the same search returns 2 hops: Duflo won the economics prize, Samuelson won the economics prize. True, and useless as a "connection". Nothing was wrong with the algorithm; the graph let it walk through a node that stands for a category, not a relationship.

The rule: decide which node and edge types count as a connection for this question, build that view, then compute. "Shortest path on the whole graph" is rarely the question anyone meant.

Self-studyWeighted paths, and SHORTEST k in Cypher4 min▶

Give edges a cost and "shortest" becomes "cheapest": pass weight="cost" to networkx and it uses Dijkstra's method instead. Weights are one way out of the prize shortcut: make a hop through a prize node expensive and the search prefers real ties, without deleting anything.

In Neo4j's current Cypher manual, shortest paths are written with the SHORTEST k, ALL SHORTEST and ANY keywords inside the pattern. The manual says SHORTEST "functionally replaces and extends" the older shortestPath() and allShortestPaths() functions, which still run but are not GQL conformant; k can be a parameter as of Neo4j 2025.06. Restricting the edge type in the pattern is the Cypher version of choosing the advisor view:

MATCH p = SHORTEST 1
  (a:Person {name:"Esther Duflo"})-[:ADVISED]-+(b:Person {name:"Paul Samuelson"})
RETURN p

Run it in Neo4j against your own load of the extract; the query box on these pages does not support SHORTEST, so no output is shown here. Swap [:ADVISED] for an untyped [] and you have asked the full-graph question again.

Real world

A loop in Wikidata. Theodor Zincke lists Otto Hahn as both his doctoral advisor and his doctoral student, while Hahn lists Zincke as his advisor. So the graph holds Hahn advised Zincke and Zincke advised Hahn: a two-node loop. A shortest path never uses a loop, but anything that counts paths does: "how many academic descendants within three generations" walks Hahn to Zincke to Hahn and counts it. The undirected views in tonight's widget collapse the pair into one edge, which hides the artefact instead of fixing it. The graph is only as true as its edges.

Part 2 · covers degree, betweenness, PageRank and the hub trap

Three ways to be central

5 min live

"Most connected" is three different questions. Degree counts neighbours. Betweenness counts how many shortest paths run through you. PageRank asks whether your neighbours are themselves important. On the advisor network they agree at the top on Ernest Rutherford, and disagree on everyone who matters as a bridge.

LiveWhat each measure answers3 min▶
MeasureThe question it answersAdvisor network winner
DegreeWho has the most direct ties? networkx's degree_centrality divides by n - 1, the most ties possible; the widget shows the raw count.Ernest Rutherford, 15
BetweennessHow many shortest paths between other pairs run through you? betweenness_centrality normalises by (n-1)(n-2)/2 on an undirected graph, so 0.0709 is a share of all pairs.August Kundt, 0.0709
PageRankIf you wander the graph at random, how often are you here? A tie to a central node counts more than a tie to a leaf. pagerank(G, alpha=0.85, ...); alpha is the damping, the chance of following an edge rather than jumping anywhere.Ernest Rutherford, 0.0051

Read degree and PageRank together and they mostly agree: Rutherford, Thomson, Leontief and Sommerfeld sit in both top fives. PageRank lets Enrico Fermi in, because his students and advisors are themselves well connected. Betweenness tells a different story entirely.

LiveWhy bridges are not stars3 min▶

August Kundt and Georg Hermann Quincke top betweenness, 0.0709 and 0.0704, and neither won a Nobel Prize. Of the betweenness top five only Gabriel Lippmann is a laureate. They are in the graph because laureates studied under them or under their students, and they score highest because they join branches of the academic family tree that would otherwise be far apart: many shortest paths between two large groups of physicists have to pass through them.

Degree misses them because a bridge does not need many ties, only the right ones. A person with a handful of students in two different lineages can outrank a famous teacher with fifteen students all in one lineage. That is the useful finding for an editor: the "most connected" scientist by one count is a different person from the one who connects the field.

LiveThe hub trap: a country wins every measure3 min▶

Switch the widget to the full graph, with institutions, countries and prizes as nodes. United States wins degree with 629 neighbours, betweenness with 0.3657 and PageRank with 0.0245. The prize nodes follow: Physiology or Medicine with 235 neighbours, Physics with 230, and the Physics prize is second on betweenness at 0.1174.

None of this is influence. It is the modelling choice from session 2: citizenship and prizes were stored as nodes, so every citizen and every winner hangs off one hub. A hub with hundreds of spokes puts any two of its neighbours two hops apart, so it sits on a huge share of all shortest paths, and it collects PageRank from everyone who points at it. The same hub swallows communities: label propagation on the full graph finds 42, and the largest, 1,521 nodes, is led by United States.

The fix is a modelling decision, made before you compute: run centrality on the view that matches the question (the advisor network for "who connects scientists"), or drop the hub types, or down-weight edges into them.

A centrality ranking is a statement about the view, not the world. Before you publish a "most central" list, check the kind of each top node. If the winners are countries, categories or prize nodes, the list is measuring your schema. Name the view in the same sentence as the number: "on the advisor network, August Kundt has the highest betweenness".

Self-studyComputing cost, Brandes, and checking the widget4 min▶

Betweenness is the expensive one. The standard method, Brandes's algorithm, runs one breadth-first search from every node and then walks back through each search tree, crediting every node with its share of the shortest paths that passed through it. One search per node is why the widget warns that betweenness on the full graph takes a second or two, while degree is instant. networkx's betweenness_centrality(G, k=None, normalized=True, ...) takes a k to estimate from a sample of source nodes when the graph is too big.

The widget is not a picture of networkx; it computes everything in plain JavaScript in your browser. It was checked against networkx on both views: betweenness matches to 1e-16, and PageRank, run for 100 fixed iterations, lands within 7e-6 of networkx's result. The algorithms are short enough to read in an afternoon; the hard part is never the arithmetic, it is choosing the view.

One subtlety: these are undirected views. ADVISED is directed (advisor to student), but "who connects the field" treats a tie as a tie. A directed PageRank on advisor-to-student edges would answer a different question, closer to "whose lineage is deepest".

Real world

PageRank started as a ranking of the web. Page, Brin, Motwani and Winograd described it in "The PageRank Citation Ranking: Bringing Order to the Web", a Stanford InfoLab technical report dated November 1999 (reported: we found it through bibliographic records; the report server would not load). The idea carries straight over: a page, or a scientist, matters if things that matter point to it. So does the trap: a link farm on the web and a country node in a knowledge graph both manufacture importance from structure.

Part 3 · covers label propagation, Louvain and Leiden

Communities are a modelling output, not a fact

5 min live

A community is a group of nodes with many edges inside and few edges out. Algorithms disagree on where the borders go, and on how many groups there are. Run two of them on the same advisor network and you get 216 communities from one and 159 from the other. Neither number is wrong. Both are answers to slightly different questions.

LiveWhat a community is, and modularity in one paragraph3 min▶

Informally, a community is a set of nodes more tightly connected to each other than to the rest. Modularity turns that into a score for a whole partition: the fraction of edges that fall inside communities, minus the fraction you would expect inside them if the same nodes kept their degrees but the edges were wired at random. Positive means "more clustered than chance". Louvain and Leiden search for a partition with high modularity; networkx's greedy_modularity_communities does the same greedily, using the Clauset, Newman and Moore method (Physical Review E, 2004).

Label propagation does not optimise any score. Every node starts with its own label and repeatedly adopts the label most common among its neighbours, until nothing changes. Labels spread through dense regions and stall at sparse borders. networkx's label_propagation_communities is the semi-synchronous version (Cordasco and Gargano, 2010). The widget uses a simpler variant, and says so: asynchronous, nodes updated one at a time in a fixed order, ties broken toward the smallest label. That makes it deterministic, which is why it gives 216 communities after 7 rounds every time you load the page.

LiveDifferent algorithms, different counts3 min▶

On the advisor network the widget's label propagation finds 216 communities. networkx's Louvain, run with seed 1 on exactly the same view, finds 159. Same nodes, same edges, a different number of groups. The advisor network also has 145 connected components, pieces with no path between them at all, so every algorithm must return at least 145 groups; the rest is a choice about how finely to split each piece.

Teach the count as a modelling output, like the number of clusters in k-means. If the editors ask "how many schools of physics were there?", the honest answer names the algorithm and its settings, and shows that the large groups are stable across methods even when the small ones are not. The five largest communities are a far safer thing to publish than the total.

Self-studyResolution, randomness and Leiden4 min▶

networkx's louvain_communities(G, weight='weight', resolution=1, threshold=1e-07, max_level=None, seed=None) has two levers worth knowing. resolution sets the preferred community size: above 1 favours more, smaller communities, below 1 fewer, larger ones. seed matters because Louvain visits nodes in a random shuffled order; without a seed the partition, and the count, can change between runs. The 159 above is for seed 1. Louvain arrived in networkx 2.7, released on 28 February 2022.

Leiden (Traag, Waltman and van Eck, 2019) fixes a flaw the authors found in Louvain: it "may yield arbitrarily badly connected communities", including communities that are not even connected inside. Leiden adds a refinement step that guarantees connected communities, and the paper reports it is also faster. Microsoft's GraphRAG applies Leiden hierarchically to build its community summaries, which is where session 6 picks it up.

import networkx as nx
parts = nx.community.louvain_communities(G_advisor, seed=1)
len(parts)          # compare with the widget's label propagation
nx.community.modularity(G_advisor, parts)

Run it yourself on the advisor view; try seeds 1 to 5 and two resolutions, and record how much the count moves.

Real world

From a phone network to a warning. Blondel and colleagues introduced Louvain in 2008 and tested it on a network of 2.6 million mobile phone customers and on a web graph of 118 million nodes. Eleven years later Traag and colleagues showed the same method can return communities that are badly connected inside, and proposed Leiden. The arc is the lesson: a method can be fast, famous and useful for a decade and still return groups you should not take at face value.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why does August Kundt top betweenness on the advisor network when he is not in the degree top five?

Betweenness counts the shortest paths between other pairs that run through a node. Kundt joins branches of the academic family tree, so he scores 0.0709 without many ties. He is not a laureate, and the widget's betweenness matches networkx to 1e-16.

2 · On the full graph, United States tops degree (629), betweenness (0.3657) and PageRank (0.0245). What does that tell Vellum?

A node with hundreds of spokes puts any two of its neighbours two hops apart, so it sits on a huge share of all shortest paths and collects rank from everyone. The fix is choosing the view (the advisor network) or dropping or down-weighting hub types, not tuning the algorithm.

3 · Label propagation finds 216 communities on the advisor network and Louvain finds 159. Which is right?

Label propagation spreads the most common neighbour label; Louvain greedily maximises modularity and depends on its seed and resolution. Different objectives give different borders on the same edges. Neither is a fact about the world.
