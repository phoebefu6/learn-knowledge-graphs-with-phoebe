GraphRAG: when the graph feeds the LLM

A graph earns its keep twice: once when a query walks it, and again when what it found becomes the context a language model reads. Tonight we look at the two simple ways a graph reaches a prompt, at Microsoft's GraphRAG and what it costs to build, and at the evaluations that disagree about when any of it beats plain retrieval. Part 1 · covers graph as context: subgraph as facts, text-to-Cypher

Two ways a graph reaches the prompt

5 min live

The simplest graph RAG is not a framework. Either your code walks the graph and hands the model the path it found, written as plain facts, or the model writes the query itself and reads the rows that come back. Both are local: they start from an entity the question names.

LiveSerialising a path as facts3 min▶

Pattern A is the bench's graph method with a model on the end. Your code anchors on the entity the question names, runs a fixed pattern, and writes each edge on the answer path as one short sentence: "Abhijit Banerjee advised Esther Duflo." The model reads a handful of facts that are, by construction, the evidence for the answer.

That is the cost argument in miniature. On session 4's bench the graph method used 4.0 facts per question on average and found all 24 questions. Keyword chunks at k = 10 sent 174 words per question and found 9. Short context is cheaper to send and easier to check: if the answer cites a fact, you can point to the edge it came from.

Two habits keep it honest. Serialise the direction you stored (advisor to student), so the model never has to guess who advised whom. And keep the QID next to each name in a hidden field, so two people sharing a label stay apart.

LiveText-to-Cypher, and the silent zero3 min▶

Pattern B lets the model write the query. Neo4j's Text2Cypher paper (Ozsoy et al., 2024) combined and cleaned public datasets into 44,387 question and query pairs and reports gains from fine-tuning, measured in Google-BLEU and exact match. The dataset's card is reported to split them 39,554 for training and 4,833 for test.

The failure that matters is not a syntax error, which you would see. It is a valid query with one wrong literal. Graph patterns match names exactly, so "Max Delbruck" without the umlaut is a perfectly good query that returns nothing. The model then answers from zero rows, often fluently. This is session 4's trap in a new place: a missing edge and a misspelled anchor both look like "no answer", and neither raises an error.

The discipline is the one text-to-SQL already learned: link the question's words to real schema items and real values before generating. See Text-to-SQL, schema linking ↗; for a graph, the "values" step means resolving names to QIDs first, and treating 0 rows as "check the anchor", never as "none".

Self-studyRead-only credentials, always3 min▶

A model that writes queries can write a delete. LangChain's GraphCypherQAChain carries a security note asking for database "credentials that are narrowly-scoped to only include necessary permissions", and warns that broad credentials risk data corruption, loss or exposure. Its allow_dangerous_requests flag is reported to default to False, so the chain refuses to run until you opt in by name.

Opting in is not the fix; the database user is. Give the chain a role that can only read the labels and relationship types the desk needs. Then the worst a bad generation can do is return the wrong rows, which your 0-row and row-count checks can catch.

Real world

A public benchmark for the model-writes-the-query route. Neo4j published its Text2Cypher data on Hugging Face as neo4j/text2cypher-2024v1 under the Apache-2.0 licence. It is the natural place to test a prompt or a fine-tune before pointing it at your own graph, and a reminder that exact match on the query is a stricter test than "it ran".

Part 2 · covers Microsoft GraphRAG and LazyGraphRAG

GraphRAG: summaries for the whole corpus

5 min live

Microsoft's GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2024) answers a different kind of question. Not "which prize did Duflo's grand-advisor win?" but "what are the main themes across these thousand documents?" No single chunk holds that answer, and no single entity anchors it. So GraphRAG builds the graph from the text with an LLM, groups it into communities, and summarises every community in advance.

LiveWhat "global" questions are, and why vector RAG misses them4 min▶

Edge and colleagues target "global sensemaking" questions: ones about the whole corpus, such as its main themes. Retrieval of the top-k most similar chunks cannot answer them well, because the answer is spread across hundreds of chunks and none of them is "about" the question more than the others.

Their pipeline, read at source: chunk the documents; have an LLM extract entities, relationships and claims into a graph; partition the graph with Leiden (Traag et al., 2019), applied hierarchically until the leaf communities, using the graspologic library; have an LLM write a summary of every community; then answer a question by map-reduce. In the map step each community summary produces a partial answer in parallel; the reduce step combines them.

The data: a Podcast set of about 1 million tokens in 1,669 chunks of 600 tokens, and a News set of about 1.7 million tokens in 3,197 chunks. Against vector RAG, GraphRAG's answers won on comprehensiveness 72-83% of the time on Podcast and 72-80% on News, and on diversity 75-82% and 62-71%. Vector RAG produced the most direct answers in all comparisons. The root-level summaries (C0) needed "over 97% fewer tokens" than summarising the source text directly.

The cost sits at index time: building the graph for the Podcast set took 281 minutes with gpt-4-turbo. And the authors' own limitation: two corpora of about 1 million tokens each.

Self-studyThe judge's four criteria3 min▶

Edge et al. score answers head to head with an LLM judge, 125 questions per comparison, each repeated five times, on four criteria:

CriterionWhat it rewardsResult vs vector RAG
ComprehensivenessHow much of the question's ground the answer coversGraphRAG ahead
DiversityHow many different perspectives it offersGraphRAG ahead
EmpowermentHow well it helps the reader judge for themselvesMixed
DirectnessHow specifically it answers; used as a validity checkVector RAG ahead

Notice what is not on the list: whether the answer is correct against a known key. That is reasonable for "main themes", which has no key, and it is exactly where Part 3's critics push.

Self-studyLazyGraphRAG's trade2 min▶

Microsoft Research's LazyGraphRAG (blog, 25 November 2024) moves the LLM out of indexing. It extracts noun phrases with ordinary NLP instead of an LLM, so indexing costs are "identical to vector RAG and 0.1% of the costs of full GraphRAG", and it defers LLM work to query time. The blog reports global-query quality comparable to GraphRAG's global search at "more than 700 times lower query cost".

The trade is the usual one: pay up front for summaries every query can reuse, or pay per query for work done only when someone asks. If your corpus changes daily and global questions are rare, lazy wins. If the corpus is stable and global questions are the product, the up-front index may pay back.

Real world

Chunk size changes the graph. In the same paper, GPT-4 extracted almost twice as many entity references from 600-token chunks as from 2,400-token chunks on a HotpotQA sample. Smaller chunks mean more extraction calls, and more of the graph. The graph an LLM builds is a function of how you fed it, which is session 4's lesson about extraction coverage in another form.

Part 3 · covers the contested evidence: Han, Xiang, Zhou

Where the evaluations disagree

5 min live

GraphRAG's gains are contested, and the honest position is to hold the disagreement rather than pick a side. Three independent evaluations from 2025 agree on some cells of the map and disagree on others, and the disagreements have reasons you can name: what was judged, by whom, and how much of the graph existed.

LiveWhere the evaluations agree3 min▶

Single-hop facts go to plain retrieval. Han et al. (2025, a systematic RAG vs GraphRAG evaluation) find "RAG excels on detailed single-hop queries". Xiang et al. (2025, GraphRAG-Bench) find "basic RAG is comparable to or outperforms GraphRAG in simple fact retrieval tasks". Two independent groups, same row.

Multi-hop leans to graphs. Han et al. find GraphRAG methods perform best on the multi-hop benchmarks, for example HippoRAG2 at 63.01 F1 against RAG's 60.04 on HotpotQA. Xiang et al. find a clear advantage for GraphRAG in complex reasoning and contextual summarisation. Note the size: three F1 points, not a rout.

Routing helps. Han et al. report that selecting or combining RAG and GraphRAG per query gave consistent gains. Zhou et al. (2025), who put graph-based RAG methods in one framework, find different variants win on specific and on abstract questions. Nobody's evidence says one method for every question.

LiveWhere they disagree, and why4 min▶

The judge. Edge et al. used an LLM judge and found GraphRAG more comprehensive. Han et al., also judging summaries with an LLM, prefer RAG for comprehensiveness and GraphRAG's global search for diversity, and report that "position bias is clearly present": swap the order the two answers are shown in and verdicts can flip. A head-to-head judged by a model is a measurement with its own error bar.

Coverage. Han et al. find KG-based GraphRAG generally underperforms because of limited graph coverage, and that community global search can lose the fine-grained evidence detail questions need. A graph only answers from the edges it holds.

Cost. Xiang et al. find GraphRAG "significantly increases prompt length": Microsoft GraphRAG's global search around 4x10^4 tokens per query against about 10^3 for HippoRAG2. A quality gain bought with forty times the prompt is a different decision from a free one.

Which of these dominates depends on your questions, your judge and your graph. That is why the capstone classifies the desk's questions before choosing anything. RAG evaluation itself, judges and their biases included, is taught in RAG, evaluation ↗.

Session 4 measured the same shape on Vellum's graph, with no judge at all: a question counts as found only when every fact on its answer path was retrieved.

Read it alongside the papers. Chunks tie the graph on one hop, as Han and Xiang would predict. The graph wins the multi-hop rows, as they also predict. And the third row is Han's coverage finding made concrete: drop one relation type at extraction time and the graph falls below the chunks, reporting nothing wrong. The bench's baseline is single-shot keyword retrieval; vectors and hybrid search, which live in RAG, metadata and hybrid retrieval ↗, would move the chunk numbers, not the trap.

Real world

Multi-hop benchmarks are where the graph rows were measured. HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018) holds 113k Wikipedia questions that require reasoning over multiple supporting documents; MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022) composed 25K questions of two to four hops from single-hop ones. When a paper reports a GraphRAG gain, check which benchmark it came from: a multi-hop gain says little about your single-hop help-desk traffic.

Neo4j Desktop is free and runs on Linux, Mac and Windows. AuraDB Free needs no credit card; its current quotas page lists 200,000 nodes, 400,000 relationships and 1GB of storage. Older pages still quote smaller limits; the quotas page is the current figure. Vellum's extract, 3,507 nodes and 10,954 edges, fits with room to spare.

Self-studyTry GraphRAG yourself30 min▶

Microsoft's open-source GraphRAG implementation is linked from the Edge et al. paper. Run it on a small corpus you know well, so you can judge the community summaries yourself. Before indexing, estimate the calls: one extraction call per chunk, plus one summary call per community. Note how long indexing takes and how long the global prompt is; compare with the published reference points in step 3, and expect yours to differ.

Then ask it one single-hop factual question and one global question. Per Han et al. and Xiang et al., you would expect the first to be no better than plain retrieval and the second to be where it earns its keep. Check whether your corpus agrees.

The cheapest graph is the one somebody already curated. Most GraphRAG cost is the LLM turning text into edges. Vellum skipped that by starting from Wikidata. Before paying for extraction, look for a structured source of your relations: a CRM, a ticket system's links, a catalogue's lineage. Extract only what no system already records.

Homework

After the last session

45 min
  1. Classify twenty real questions your team asks into single-hop fact, multi-hop, global summary and free text. Write the share of each; that ratio is most of your decision.
  2. Write ten canaries for one graph you own or plan: known edges that must come back from a query. Run them after the next data refresh and record how many return.
  3. Find the curated source. For your multi-hop questions, list which relations already exist as structured data and which would need LLM extraction. Only the second list carries GraphRAG's indexing cost.
  4. Write your own section 6. Graph, vectors or both, with a cost side that quotes only published numbers or your own measurements, three named risks with checks, and a 90-day list.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · In Microsoft's GraphRAG, what are the community summaries for?

Edge et al. target global sensemaking questions that no single chunk answers. Each community summary answers in part (map), and the partial answers are combined (reduce). The summaries cost a call per community at index time, paid before the first question.

2 · Where do Han et al. find plain RAG does better than GraphRAG?

"RAG excels on detailed single-hop queries", while GraphRAG methods do best on multi-hop benchmarks (HippoRAG2 63.01 F1 against RAG's 60.04 on HotpotQA). They also found that choosing or combining the two per query gave consistent gains.

3 · On session 4's bench, a graph that never extracted ADVISED found 4 of 24 questions. What does that mean for GraphRAG?

GraphRAG builds its graph by LLM extraction. If a relation is never extracted, the graph answers fewer questions without any error, the same mechanism Han et al. describe as limited coverage. Canaries, known edges that must come back, are the test.
