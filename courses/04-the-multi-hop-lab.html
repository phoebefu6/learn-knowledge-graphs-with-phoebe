The multi-hop lab

Sessions 1 to 3 claimed that chunk retrieval loses the chain after the first hop and that a graph keeps it. Tonight that claim gets numbers. The bench asks 24 real questions, six at each depth from one to four hops, and answers every one twice in your browser: by walking the graph, and by retrieving the top k text chunks. Then it breaks the graph the way real extraction breaks it, and shows you what the failure looks like. It looks like nothing.

Part 1 · covers how each method answers, and what counts as found

Two answers to every question

5 min live

The graph side runs a pattern from the question's named person along typed edges and returns rows. The chunk side scores every chunk against the question's words with BM25 and keeps the top k. The rule for "found" is the same strict one on both sides: the complete answer, with its evidence.

LiveThe graph side: a pattern, a walk, rows3 min▶

Each question carries its pattern in the small Cypher subset from session 3, for example MATCH (p:Person {name:"Esther Duflo"})<-[:ADVISED*3]-(b)-[:WON]->(z:Prize) RETURN z. The engine starts at the named person and walks only edges of the named types, never reusing an edge within one path, as Cypher does. A question counts as found only when the rows returned are exactly the full answer: three of Max Planck's students won, so returning two is not found.

The hop limit lever stops the walk after h edges from the anchor. It stands for retrievers that only fetch an entity's immediate neighbourhood. The edge type never extracted lever deletes one relation type from the graph before the walk, which is what a missed extraction does.

LiveThe chunk side: BM25, top k, and the strict found rule3 min▶

Every fact becomes a sentence: "Max Born won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1954." An advisor fact appears on both people's pages, as it would in two biographies. Each person's sentences are cut into chunks of F facts (1, 2, 4 or 8). BM25 scores each chunk with Lucene's settings (k1 = 1.2, b = 0.75, Lucene's IDF), ties broken by chunk order, and the top k are kept.

A question is found when every fact on its answer path is in those k chunks. The table also shows "k needed": the smallest k that would have found it. For the four-hop Duflo question it is 350. That number is the honest version of "just retrieve more".

Self-studyWhat is generated, what is real, what is simplified4 min▶

Real: every entity and edge, from Wikidata. Generated: the sentences, from templates, so the text is cleaner than any real biography: one fact per sentence, every name spelled the Wikidata way. Real text would make the chunk side worse (pronouns, the bridge person called "his supervisor") and the graph side harder to build (extraction has to find those facts first).

Simplified: the chunk side is single-shot keyword retrieval. Embedding retrieval, hybrid search and rerankers belong to the RAG course ↗; a multi-step retriever that re-queries with "Abhijit Banerjee" after reading hop 1 would recover some deeper questions. The tokeniser keeps only a to z and digits, so "Delbrück" becomes two tokens, "delbr" and "ck"; it still ranks Delbrück's chunks first.

Real world

Why a strict "found". Han and colleagues (2025), comparing RAG and GraphRAG systematically, report that LLM-as-judge verdicts on summaries show clear position bias: swap the order of two answers and the verdict can flip. A lenient judge rewards a plausible answer. Counting the evidence path, as the bench does, cannot be talked round.

Real world

Chunk size cuts both ways in GraphRAG too. Edge and colleagues (Microsoft, 2024) report that with 600-token chunks GPT-4 extracted almost twice as many entity references as with 2,400-token chunks. Bigger chunks are cheaper to process and lose facts at extraction; smaller ones keep facts and cost more calls. The same trade shows up on the bench's retrieval side.

Part 3 · covers the missing edge and silent failure

The edge nobody extracted

5 min live

A graph is only as complete as its extraction. Remove the ADVISED relation, as if the pipeline never learned to pull "doctoral advisor" out of biographies, and the graph drops from 24 questions to 4. Nothing errors. The queries run, return zero rows, and look exactly like questions whose true answer is "none".

LiveWhy the graph fails silently, and chunks do not fail this way3 min▶

A query engine has no idea which edges ought to exist. Asked for a path that is not in the graph, it correctly returns nothing. Hogan and colleagues name the stance a graph should be read under: the open world assumption, where a missing edge means "not known", not "false". Most applications quietly treat it as false.

The chunk side is untouched by the trap: the sentences about advisors are still in the corpus, so it still finds 9. That is the real asymmetry. Text keeps facts nobody modelled; a graph only keeps what extraction put in. The ADVISED trap removes 966 edges, 9 percent of the graph, and 20 of the 24 answers.

The cost of a missing relation is set by your question set, not by the size of the relation. That is the argument for session 1's question sort: it tells you which relations your extraction must not miss, before you pay for extraction.

Real world

Coverage is the known weak point. In Han and colleagues' 2025 evaluation, knowledge-graph-based GraphRAG generally underperformed, which the authors put down to limited graph coverage: facts the questions needed were never in the graph. The bench's trap is the same failure, made small enough to see.

Keep a canary set. The trap is cheap to catch if you keep a handful of questions whose answers you know, one per relation type, and run them after every extraction job. A drop from 1 row to 0 on a canary is the only alarm a silent graph will ever give you.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Raising k from 10 to 30 at two facts per chunk changes chunk recall how?

The facts the deeper questions need rank in the hundreds, far below 30. Tripling k triples what an LLM must read and finds nothing new. 4 to 9 is k = 3 to k = 10; 15 is the grid's best cell at 8 facts per chunk and k = 100.

2 · With ADVISED edges never extracted, the graph finds 4 of 24. What does a failing query return?

The engine cannot know an edge ought to exist, so it correctly returns nothing. That is why a missing extraction is silent, and why canary questions with known answers are the alarm you have to build yourself.

3 · The graph at a one-hop limit (R4) finds 6 of 24, fewer than chunks at k = 10. What does that show?

At one hop the graph answers exactly the six one-hop questions, as chunks do; chunks at k = 10 also catch three of the two-hop ones. Raise the limit to 2, 3 and 4 and the graph finds 12, 18 and 24. The value is in the traversal, not the storage.
